Fake tools

Last checked 3 November 20267 minutes to read

A fake tool is an extension or app that does more than its page says, or makes stopping hard. In my experience few are malware, and most are working code with a hidden price.

VerifiedChecked against the original source.ReportedStated by a serious secondary source read in full.Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

In 30 seconds

  • A tool can send, reach or change more than its page says, and it stays on your computer until you remove it.
  • Most harm comes from design: no cap, no running total, no stop that always works.
  • A tool that is harmless today and could turn harmful after one update needs a different verdict from “malware”.
  • Remote control, background buying and tools that act as you are the highest risks.
  • A feature built to make the next payment harder to refuse is a reason to leave.

A tool is a promise written in code. Some promises are broken, and some are kept and still cost you more than you meant to pay. I will show you the shapes they take, so that you can say no before the install and not after the bill. To check one, use Check a tool before you install it.

What a tool can do that its page does not say

In this wiki a fake tool is any extension or app that sends more than it says, acts beyond what you agreed, or makes stopping hard. In my experience few of them are malware, and most are working code with a hidden price or no way out. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

A tool outruns its page in four ways. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  • It sends more. A tool can build a machine identifier from the names of your computer and your account, send it to its server and show it on screen. That exposes who you are. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.
  • It reaches further. A tool that keeps your signed-in session and buys in the background removes the pause between decisions. The only brake left is a cap you set beforehand. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.
  • It changes later. An updater can fetch new files after you have stopped paying attention. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.
  • It skips consent. A roleplay app can look grand and still have no safe word, no consent screen and no aftercare, and some of its promised content may never arrive. A dom who uses consent tools shows them: a stop control, a log of what she can see, and a clear line between teasing and harming. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

VVivian’s field notes

A tool that stays installed is a standing risk. If you ignore it today and its maker pushes a harmful update tomorrow, you are exposed, and a maker could wait until many people have installed it. Removing the tool is the simplest exit, and a person who has removed it cannot be drained by it.

Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Not malicious today

Calling a tool malware before reading all of it can produce a false alarm that has to be taken back. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. The sequence below is an invented example, because a correction is more useful than an alarm.

A verdict that matches what was read (invented example)

  1. 1

    First reading

    A quick look at a control app suggests the worst: stored sign-in tokens and a link to a remote server. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  2. 2

    Full reading

    Reading the whole source, updater and every network call included, shows what is really sent and to whom. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  3. 3

    The finding

    If the updater can push any file and the tokens sit on the same computer, one update could take everything. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  4. 4

    The wording

    The honest verdict names what was read and what could change: harmless today, risky after one update. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

A finding like that is narrower than “malware” and still serious. A design risk is the usual kind of danger, and it needs a verdict worded to match. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Check a tool before you install it gives the ladder of words.

  • Myth

    If a tool is not malware, it is fine.

    Fact

    A tool can be free of malware and still send identifying details about your computer to its server. A tool can be harmless in code and unsafe in use. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  • Myth

    A tool has a stop if you can close the program.

    Fact

    Some tools can only be stopped by force-closing the app or turning the computer off. A stop that depends on your willpower is no stop. Force-closing always works, so know it, but a visible stop that works in every state must also exist. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Designs that push you to spend

A capcapA limit on spending, set by the person who pays, in a calm moment, before the session. It is held outside the moment, in a server that enforces it, in a bank limit or with a trusted person, and it is raised only after a cooldown. A cap typed in the middle of a session is weak, and a cap is a ceiling, never a target. set in advance is your first brake. The features below work against it, and each makes the next payment harder to refuse. Red flags

Designs to refuse

  • A forced respin, or a prompt that cannot be closed without choosing.
  • Minimum amounts that rise after each round.
  • A sentence you must type to continue.
  • A mascot that sulks, scolds or pleads until you act.
  • A hidden total, or a charged amount that differs from the one shown.
  • A lock that releases only on payment, or a tool that cannot be removed.
  • Overnight modes, or targeting of payday and of your balance.

Counter. Close it and leave. These features exist to earn from your next send. Assume a tool has none of the protections below until you see them.

Remote control and tools that act as you

A remote-sessionremote sessionA session in which the dom controls the sub’s computer through remote-desktop software. It is a red line of this wiki, because it hands over the screen, the accounts and the saved logins, and removes the sub’s own ability to interrupt. A US cyber-security agency advises people not to give control of their computers or phones to a stranger. A remote-access vendor advises users not to give device access to people they do not know or trust. If access was already given, close the app, cut the connection, change the passwords from another device and ask a person you trust or the service concerned for help. hands your computer to someone else, and a US government alert advises people not to give control of their computers or mobile devices to any stranger. VerifiedChecked against the original source.IC3 Issues Alert on Tech Support Fraud (opens in a new tab) Accessed 3 November 2026. The full red line, with the pressure lines to refuse, is in Check a tool before you install it.

A hub-style app can add remote actions: making accounts follow, post or send, sending files, or reaching a terminal. If it does, one flaw lets the author, or anyone, act through every installed copy. An app like that deserves an independent security check before release, and a person who finds such a flaw should tell the maker and not use it. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Red flags

Tools that act as you

  • It likes, follows or posts from your accounts.
  • It keeps your signed-in session and buys in the background.
  • It is advertised as getting around your bank’s confirmation step.
  • It asks you to hand over a password, a login code or a connection code.

Counter. Refuse. Automation on your account can get it banned and can flag the dom’s account for botting, and the consequence lands on you. A version built to defeat your own bank’s check removes a brake that protects your money.

Protection that is not protection

VVivian’s field notes

Makers encrypt images and scramble code to keep copies away. The key and the logic still ship inside the app, so a person with patience, ordinary tools or an assistant can read all of it. Whatever your computer can open, someone can extract. Scrambling deters casual copying and does nothing against a determined reader. The same openness is your advantage: a tool you can inspect is a tool you can check, so I prefer them.

Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

A build step can change what a tool does, so the build that ships may differ from the one that was tested. A wrong version can charge an amount different from the one it shows. A test switch that skips the payment step, a bypassbypassA switch in a test build that skips the payment step or the draining effect so that a maker can try a tool safely. It belongs in test builds only and must be removed before release, so a tool that ships with one deserves distrust., must never leave the maker’s computer. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Makers: the list is in Check a tool before you install it.

A tool can also trap the people it is meant to serve. A bug, an overlay or a lock can leave a sub with no way to remove a tool. A lock that opens only on payment is a paid exit and a reason to leave, because it turns a game you agreed to into a harm you did not. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. A maker needs an automatic unlock and no overlay that covers the stop.
