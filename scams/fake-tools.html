Step 1 of 5 What a tool can do that its page doesn’t say

In 30 seconds

  • A tool can send, reach or change more than its page says, and it stays on your computer until you remove it.
  • Most of the harm comes from design: a tool without a limit, a running total or a stop that always works.
  • A tool that’s harmless today and could turn harmful after one update needs a different verdict from “malware”.
  • Remote control, buying in the background and tools that act as you are the biggest risks.
  • A feature built to make the next payment harder to refuse is a reason to leave.

What a tool can do that its page doesn’t say

In this wiki a fake tool is any extension or app that sends more than it says, acts beyond what you agreed to, or makes it hard to stop. In my experience few of them are malware, and most are working code with a hidden price or no way out. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

A tool can go further than its page in four ways. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  • It sends more. A tool can build a machine identifier from your computer’s name and your account name, send it to its server and show it on screen. That exposes who you are. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.
  • It reaches further. A tool that keeps your signed-in session and buys in the background takes away the pause between decisions. The only brake left is a limit you set beforehand. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.
  • It changes later. An updater can fetch new files after you’ve stopped paying attention. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.
  • It skips consent. A roleplay app can look impressive and still lack a safe word, a consent screen and aftercare, and some of its promised content may never arrive. A dom who uses consent tools shows them: a stop control, a log of what she can see, and a clear line between teasing and harming. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

VVivian’s field notes

A tool that stays installed stays a risk. If you ignore it today and its maker pushes a harmful update tomorrow, you’re exposed, and a maker could wait until lots of people have installed it. Removing the tool is the simplest way out, and once it’s gone it can’t drain you.

Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Not malicious today

Calling a tool malware before reading all of it can lead to a false alarm you have to take back. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. The sequence below is an invented example, because a correction teaches more than an alarm.

A finding like that is narrower than “malware” and still serious. A design risk is the usual kind of danger, and it needs a verdict worded to match. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Check a tool before you install it gives the words to use, from mildest to strongest.

  • Myth

    If a tool isn’t malware, it’s fine.

    Fact

    A tool can be free of malware and still send identifying details about your computer to its server. Its code can be harmless and it can still be unsafe to use. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  • Myth

    A tool has a stop if you can close the program.

    Fact

    Some tools can only be stopped by force-closing the app or turning the computer off. A stop that depends on your willpower is no stop. Force-closing always works, so know how to do it, but a visible stop that works in every state has to exist too. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Designs that push you to spend

A limitlimitA spending limit picked by the person who pays, while calm, before the session. It’s held outside your own head, in a server that enforces it, a bank limit or a trusted person, and it only goes up after a cooldown. A limit typed in the middle of a session is weak, and a limit is a ceiling, never a target. set in advance is your first brake. The features below work against it, and each one makes the next payment harder to refuse. Red flags

Designs to refuse

  • A forced respin, or a prompt you can’t close without choosing.
  • Minimum amounts that go up after each round.
  • A sentence you have to type to continue.
  • A mascot that sulks, scolds or pleads until you act.
  • A hidden total, or a charged amount that’s different from the one shown.
  • A lock that only opens on payment, or a tool that can’t be removed.
  • Overnight modes, or targeting your payday and your balance.

Counter. Close it and leave. These features are there to earn from your next send. Assume a tool has none of the protections below until you see them.

Remote control and tools that act as you

A remote-sessionremote sessionA session where the dom controls the sub’s computer through remote-desktop software. It’s a red line of this wiki, because it hands over the screen, the accounts and the saved logins, and takes away the sub’s own way to interrupt. A US cyber-security agency advises people not to give control of their computers or phones to a stranger. A remote-access vendor advises users not to give device access to people they do not know or trust. If you already gave access, close the app, cut the connection, change your passwords from another device and ask someone you trust, or the service concerned, for help. hands your computer to someone else, and a US government alert advises people not to give control of their computers or mobile devices to any stranger. VerifiedChecked against the original source.IC3 Issues Alert on Tech Support Fraud (opens in a new tab) Accessed 3 November 2026. The full rule, with the lines people use to talk you into it, is in Check a tool before you install it.

A hub-style app can add remote actions: making accounts follow, post or send, sending files, or reaching a terminal. If it does, one flaw lets the author, or anyone, act through every installed copy. An app like that needs an independent security check before release, and anyone who finds a flaw like that should tell the maker and not use it. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Red flags

Tools that act as you

  • It likes, follows or posts from your accounts.
  • It keeps your signed-in session and buys in the background.
  • It’s advertised as getting around your bank’s confirmation step.
  • It asks you to hand over a password, a login code or a connection code.

Counter. Refuse. Automation on your account can get it banned and can flag the dom’s account for botting, and you’re the one who takes the consequences. A version built to get around your own bank’s check removes a brake that protects your money.

Protection that doesn’t protect

VVivian’s field notes

Makers encrypt images and scramble code to keep copies away. The key and the logic still ship inside the app, so a person with patience, ordinary tools or an assistant can read all of it. Whatever your computer can open, someone can extract. Scrambling stops casual copying and does nothing against a determined reader. That openness works for you too: a tool you can inspect is a tool you can check, so I prefer those.

Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

A build step can change what a tool does, so the build that ships may not be the one that was tested. A wrong version can charge a different amount from the one it shows. A test switch that skips the payment step, a bypassbypassA switch in a test build that skips the payment step or the draining effect so a maker can try a tool safely. It belongs in test builds only and has to be removed before release, so don’t trust a tool that ships with one., must never leave the maker’s computer. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Makers: the list is in Check a tool before you install it.

A tool can also trap the people it’s meant to serve. A bug, an overlay or a lock can leave a sub with no way to remove a tool. A lock that only opens on payment is a paid exit and a reason to leave, because it turns a game you agreed to into a harm you didn’t. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. A maker needs an automatic unlock, and no overlay that covers the stop.
