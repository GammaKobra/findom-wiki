In 30 seconds

  • A case file follows one scam pattern from the first message to the cost and to the way out.
  • No case here is a real person or a single real event. Each is a pattern, rebuilt so that nobody can be recognised.
  • Most of the harm comes from keys, payment routes and tools, not from one villain.
  • A password, a login code or remote control of your device is never part of the game.
  • If you already sent, stop, keep the proof and tell someone you trust.

You will learn

  • The six questions every case file answers.
  • The verification fee, from the first message to the vanish.
  • Why a promise is never a safeguard when someone asks for a key to your accounts.
  • Why a payment route that is not yours can take a balance with it.
  • What to weigh before you post a warning about anyone.

A case file is a story with the names taken out. I tell each one from the side of the person who receives the message, because that is the only seat from which the pattern can still be refused. Read them once, calmly. You will know the second one on the day it arrives.

How to read a case file

Every case file answers the same six questions: what it looks like, who it targets, why it works, what it costs, what to do, and where to report it. Run them on any offer, including one that is not in this chapter.

Case 1: the verification fee

How it starts. A new account writes first, with flattering words and a set of images that looks expensive. In 2DFD the images may be stolen or generated art, so quality proves nothing about who runs the account. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Press reports fake accounts that reply to subs who post about findom and ask them to send a direct message. ReportedStated by a serious secondary source read in full.The Curse of the Fake Financial Dominatrix (opens in a new tab) Accessed 3 November 2026.

How it works (invented example)

  1. 1

    Contact

    The account writes first and moves fast.

  2. 2

    The hook

    “I only take serious subs.”

  3. 3

    The fee

    “Pay a verification fee to prove you are real.”

  4. 4

    The vanish

    The account blocks you, or asks for a second fee.

Who it targets. New subs, and anyone who has just posted about wanting a dom. The scam has a low barrier to entry. ReportedStated by a serious secondary source read in full.Financial Domination Has a Scamming Problem (opens in a new tab) Accessed 3 November 2026.

Why it works. A fee to prove that you are real proves nothing, because nobody can check what it was for. It works on the wish to be accepted and on the fear of losing the chance. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Some real dommes also ask for payment first, to keep time-wasters away. ReportedStated by a serious secondary source read in full.Financial Domination Has a Scamming Problem (opens in a new tab) Accessed 3 November 2026. The tell is a payment with no purpose except to prove that you are serious, in a form that is hard to reverse.

What it costs. The first fee, then a second ask under another name: insurance, an unlock fee, refund processing. Gift cards and crypto are hard to reverse once the funds are used. A card issuer, a bank or a gift-card company can sometimes block or reverse a payment that is reported fast, so contact them at once. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Red flags

The tells

  • Payment comes before any conversation, and it is described as proof of something.
  • The fee is asked in gift cards or crypto, not through a platform that keeps a record.
  • A timer is attached: now, or the offer is gone forever.
  • Simple personal questions get no answer in the account’s own words.

Counter. Send nothing before a real conversation. A real dom can wait an hour. The full list is in Spot a fake dom.

Where to report. On the platform where you met the account, with the messages and receipts attached. The longer path back is If you already sent.

If you already sent

  1. 1

    Stop sending, even to “unlock a refund”.

  2. 2

    Keep the messages and the payment receipts.

  3. 3

    Contact your bank, card issuer or the gift-card company at once, and ask whether the payment can be blocked.

  4. 4

    Report the account on the platform where you met.

  5. 5

    Tell someone you trust what happened.

Case 2: the access request

How it starts. A dom asks for the login of a second social account, then for the code that arrives on your phone. Sometimes it is a remote-desktop session. It is framed as proof of loyalty or as the price of keeping the bond. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Why it works. It arrives at an emotional moment, wrapped in reassurance. A spoken promise protects nothing, and a login code lets another person take over the account at once. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Public guidance tells people not to hand control of a computer or phone to a stranger. VerifiedChecked against the original source.IC3 Issues Alert on Tech Support Fraud (opens in a new tab) Accessed 3 November 2026. A remote-access vendor says the same about anyone you do not know or trust. VerifiedChecked against the original source.TeamViewer and scamming (opens in a new tab) Accessed 3 November 2026. In 2DFD nobody knows who is behind the character, so every dom is a stranger in this sense. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

What it costs. The account, the private messages in it and your name on whatever comes next. Shared logins remove the independent space a sub needs to say no or to leave. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Case 3: the tool that says one thing and does another

How it starts. A page promises a one-click helper. Everything stays local, nothing is paid unless a special mode is switched on, updates are automatic. A page is a promise. The behaviour is in the code. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Why it works. Most people read the page and not the permissions. Chrome shows a permission warning at install. It also disables an extension after an update that adds a warning-level permission, until you accept it. VerifiedChecked against the original source.Permission warnings (Chrome for Developers, extensions documentation) (opens in a new tab) Accessed 3 November 2026. Read the permission list first. Check a tool before you install it shows how, and Fake tools shows the patterns.

A worked example in reverse. A first reading of a tool can suggest the worst, and a full reading can show a narrower risk. A tool whose updater can replace any file, kept next to login tokens on the same computer, could take everything with one update. That is a different verdict from “malware”. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

VVivian’s field notes

A false alarm costs trust as much as a missed one. Read the whole source, the updater and every network call included, before you use the word malware. A tool that does no harm today and could do harm after one update is a finding, not an accusation.

Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

What it costs. Whatever the tool is allowed to reach: accounts and payments. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. What to do. Install nothing that you cannot explain in a sentence. Where to report. To the store that distributes it.

Case 4: the payment route that is not yours

How it starts. A dom collects through a payment service. Some personas misdescribe their activity to a payment service, which breaks its terms and is why balances freeze. Or the platform that carries her wishlist loses its payment partner and closes. Either way, the money sits on a route that somebody else owns. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Why it matters to you. A payment service can close an account that it finds described falsely and hold the balance, and your payment goes with it. Terms differ from one service to the next and they change. Anonymity ends where the money lands, because a payout is tied to a real identity. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Red flags

Signs that the route is shaky

  • She asks you to describe your payment as something it is not.
  • She pushes you to pay outside the platform’s visible route.
  • She avoids plain words about money and about what a gift buys.
  • She promises results that a gift cannot buy.

Counter. Say what the payment is, in plain words. If a dom asks you to say it is something else, stop. The label can freeze her money and yours.

What it costs. A frozen balance and a trail tied to a real identity. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Where to report. To the platform, if a dom asks you to mislabel a payment. A dom who needs a legitimate route should read Getting paid.

Case 6: the click-to-post chain and the display account

How it starts. A chain of links drafts a post on your own account each time you click. The drafts escalate from a playful label to a confession to a payment announcement with a link. You publish the text yourself, under your own identity. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

A close cousin is the display account: a dom takes over a sub’s public account and turns it into a showcase of his sends. Treat a request to hand over a social account as a loss of control over your own identity, not as a game feature. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Before you publish a draft that someone else wrote

  1. 1

    Read the whole draft, not only the first line.

  2. 2

    Ask whether you would sign it with your own name in a year.

  3. 3

    Do not publish from an account tied to your real identity.

  4. 4

    Decline the next link in the chain. Declining costs nothing.

Names, call-outs and proof

When you have been hurt, you may want to post a warning. A public warning can protect others, but weak evidence invites claims of defamation, and the person you name may have nothing to do with it. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. There is no foolproof way to screen out scammers. ReportedStated by a serious secondary source read in full.The Curse of the Fake Financial Dominatrix (opens in a new tab) Accessed 3 November 2026.

  • Myth

    If I know the handle, I can say who is behind the persona.

    Fact

    Linking a handle to a real person without that person’s own words is doxxingdoxTo expose a real identity or address. It is the main safety fear behind anonymity in the scene, so a request for a real name or for a gift that needs a physical address is a risk. It can strike the person behind a persona and the sub alike.. It also invites retaliation, such as mass reports from accounts the other side controls. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

A warning, or not

An account took a small amount from you and vanished. You hold the messages. A friend urges you to post the account’s name and its images on a public thread tonight.

Six questions for any offer

Ask these every time
AskWhy it matters
Can I follow the payment afterwards?A route that keeps a record for both sides protects both. Gift cards and crypto are hard to reverse once the funds are used.
Did money come before conversation?Some real doms do ask first. A payment named as proof of something is the tell.
Is there a timer?A timer exists to stop you from thinking. Wait an hour, then a day.
Does it need a key to my life?A password, a login code or remote access is never part of the game.
Would I sign this post with my own name?If not, do not publish it from your own account.
Does the tool do only what its page says?Read the permissions before you install, and remove what you cannot stop.
