No income figure for 2DFD has been measured. This chapter sorts the numbers you will hear by how far they can be trusted, and teaches the sums you can do yourself.

In 30 seconds

  • Nothing in the 2DFD scene has been measured. Every income figure is memory, a guess or a rumour.
  • The common case is small: very few subs really send, and one of them can carry most of the money.
  • A unit price hides the total. Multiply before you send.
  • Fixed fees decide which payment rail pays off, and tax changes what a share really costs.
  • Big figures are the lure. Read them last, and as hearsay.

You will learn

  • Which numbers are measured, documented, self-reported or hearsay.
  • What a small persona’s numbers look like.
  • How to compute a break-even, a real price and an hourly rate.
  • Which figures to distrust.

I will give you numbers, and I will tell you how little most of them are worth. The small ones are common and rarely spoken. The large ones are loud and mostly rumour. If you remember one thing, remember that a number you cannot trace is only a story with a price tag.

The small numbers

In my experience, the working numbers are tiny. A small persona has very few subs who really send. Here “active” means sending, not chatting. A long follower list can hold only a handful of reliable payers. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Income is concentrated and lumpy. One sub can account for a large share of a small persona’s income. A strong week can be very good, and other days bring little or nothing. The largest single sends are rare. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

A figure of about USD 1,000.00 for the first three months is passed around, and nobody has checked it. UnverifiedPlausible, not proven yet. It comes from one circle of people who stayed in touch. It says nothing about the many who left, so it flatters the average.

What it costs to run a persona

A persona costs money before she earns any. Image and video generation, subscriptions, hosting and a domain add up, and one paying sub cannot fund the tooling. One measure of a healthy position is that the sends comfortably cover the subscriptions. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. That measure counts the dom’s costs and ignores what the sub spent.

Compute a break-even before you choose a rail

  1. 1

    Write the processor’s fixed yearly fee, F.

  2. 2

    Write the commission rates: p for the processor and c for the platform.

  3. 3

    The break-even is F divided by (c minus p), in yearly sales.

  4. 4

    Invented example: F is USD 1,500.00, p is 10 percent and c is 20 percent. The break-even is USD 1,500.00 divided by 0.10, which is USD 15,000.00 a year. Below that, the platform costs less.

  5. 5

    Compare it with your honest yearly estimate, never with your best month.

Tax changes what a percentage costs. In many countries this kind of income is probably taxable, but rules vary, so ask a professional. UnverifiedPlausible, not proven yet. The arithmetic is simple. Suppose tax takes 40 percent, so you keep 60 percent. A 15 percent share promised on the gross is then 25 percent of what you keep. A cost of USD 300.00 a month needs USD 500.00 in sends.

The real price of a mechanic

A wishlist is a price menu: about ten themed rungs, from a coffee-sized gift to a showcase item, and the dom steers a sub up one rung at a time. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Other tools offer fixed tiers or a wheel. The sum to do is the same each time.

The unit price hides the total. Builders copy the pity counter from commercial games, and a guarantee after roughly 80 pulls is one I have seen discussed. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. At USD 3.00 a pull, the guaranteed route costs USD 240.00. At USD 5.00 it costs USD 400.00. The price to judge is the total, not the pull.

Two modes are common: a flat price, and a random item from the wishlist. Most subs choose the flat one, and the random mode produces single sends several times larger. A tool that runs by itself changes the scale. One gift of USD 1.00 every 6 seconds is USD 600.00 an hour. With no pause between purchases, an engine can spend a hundred gifts in a few minutes, so the pause is the main brake. A lock whose only exit is a purchase removes the free stop, and it earns by doing so. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Do the sum

  1. Question 1 of 3A design guarantees its top reward after 80 pulls at USD 3.00 a pull. What is the real price?

    Answer. USD 240.00. The guarantee is reached only after the whole run: 80 times USD 3.00.

  2. Question 2 of 3A tool sends USD 1.00 every 6 seconds. What does it send in an hour?

    Answer. USD 600.00. 3,600 seconds divided by 6 is 600 sends.

  3. Question 3 of 3A board ranks doms by the number of gifts. What does the top place show?

    Answer. The most gifts received, which tiny sends can inflate. Rankings by count are a poor measure of income. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Reach, time and asking

Reach is a number you should measure and not assume. Reach varies widely between accounts of the same creator. In my experience, for a small account the first hour of an ordinary post brings a few hundred views, a typical post one to two thousand, and a lucky spike eight to ten thousand. A repost from a bigger account is often the only lift. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Time is the other budget. A persona with part-time hours can carry far fewer subs than one working full time. Release speed hides preparation, so someone who ships often has probably prepared for months. Drawn content scales: a local setup can produce hundreds of pictures, while weak hardware waits minutes for one. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Asking is the largest lever, and it points at you. A dom who answers nearly everyone earns more than one who skips available sessions. Repeated asking is the lever a pressure tactic uses, and you may refuse it every time. If you are asked after you said stop, that is the lever, and Pressure tactics, both ways names it. Timing an ask to your payday or balance, or putting a deadline on your decision, is the same lever. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.
