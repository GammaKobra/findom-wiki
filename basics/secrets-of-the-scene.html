Step 1 of 6 Money comes from a few people

This chapter is not advice for your situation.

In 30 seconds

  • Most of the scene’s money comes from very few people, and those people come and go.
  • Warmth is part of a business that needs you to stay. It can be sincere and still steer your spending.
  • Hours of work don’t buy reach, and none of the rules for staying visible has been checked.
  • Many drawn personas are said to be run by someone other than the character, often a man. There’s no official number.
  • A serious dom shows her limits, her prices and a way to stop before she asks for money, and never needs a workaround.

You will learn

  • Why income rests on a handful of people, and why subs leave.
  • What decides who is seen, and which rules are only folklore.
  • How to read a persona’s claims about who she is.
  • Which designs and pressure tactics to recognise and refuse.
  • What a serious dom shows before she asks for anything.

Here’s what the scene keeps quiet, and it’s less glamorous than the rumours: who pays, who gets seen, who copies whom. Read it before you start, as a sub or as a dom, so you know where the risks are.

Money comes from a few people

In my experience, a persona’s income comes from very few people. One supporter can make up a third of a good week or more, and a long follower list can hold only a handful of people who send regularly. A new persona’s followers are often other doms who chat but don’t spend. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

A leaderboardleaderboardA ranking of subs by how much they sent or how far they got, kept by the dom and sometimes shown in public. A public tally turns spending into a contest, which is a pressure design to watch for, so a name should only go on one by choice. shows this and hides it at the same time. One sub with two accounts can take two of the top places, and a board that ranks by number of gifts puts a flood of tiny sends above someone who earns more. A public count of wins is advertising and proves nothing. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Subs come and go. They leave for a rival, out of boredom or curiosity, or because they regret what they did while aroused. They send every few days or in waves instead of every day, and many pay several doms at once. Some vanish after a large send or a relapserelapseSending money again after deciding to stop. A relapser is a sub who quits and comes back several times, and it’s a real warning sign of compulsive spending. One press commentator compared some cases to problem gambling. Telling someone is a first step, and Where to get help lists where to go., and some come back months later. A dom can’t stop anyone leaving. She can only make staying more appealing. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Here’s what that means for a sub. A business that lives off a few people has every reason to keep each of them close. The warmth can still be sincere. What matters is whether it’s steering your spending, and in my experience the one who pays most is under the most pressure. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Reach is partly luck

Who posts matters more than what gets posted. A post is shown to a few people first, and to more if they react. A weaker post from an account the platform favours can beat a better one from an account it doesn’t. A repost from a bigger account can lift a small one for a while. Early views tell you little: a post can sit still and then take off. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Hours of editing don’t buy reach. A plain picture with one word sometimes takes off, and a long edit sometimes sits at a few views. A brand-new account often gets pushed hard at first, and an older one with poor visibility is treated as dead. An account that’s labelled, or hidden from suggestions with an SSBSSBShort for search suggestion ban on the main social platform: the account stops showing up in search suggestions, so reach drops and a new persona struggles to grow. The long form is a guess from how the word is used, because no platform document spells it out. Checking for it every day can wear you down., loses most of its reach. What wins, and who is behind it

Simple and new usually beats polished. The tools that do best are often a simple idea nobody had bothered to build. A character people recognise and an easy setup count as much as the art. Ideas get copied within weeks, and since AI writes most of the code, nothing technical stops a copy. What a copy can’t take is the audience of the account that promotes the tool. Copying a mechanic is common, and the scene dislikes copies that don’t give credit. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Disliking a creator’s business doesn’t justify harming her.

A drawn persona can grow in a way a photographed one can’t, because pictures, voice lines and lore can be made in bulk. A fixed look makes her easy to recognise, even as a thumbnail. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Many drawn personas are run by someone other than the character, and it’s often a man. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Protect your attention

  1. 1

    Before the day starts, set a daily time limit for contacts who don’t send. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  2. 2

    Tell people who chat for free that you’re busy, and don’t sell your time short.

  3. 3

    Show your rules first: limits, prices and a way to stop.

  4. 4

    When stress takes over, pause and say so. A pause doesn’t need drama.

Pressure you may meet

Slot games with near-misses on purpose, bets that go up as the effects grow, and bets with no prize for winning are all standard. Red flags

Designs that look like fun

  • The reel stops one step short of the prize, again and again.
  • A bet has no prize at all for winning.
  • A tool copies a pity counter and banners from a commercial game.
  • The only way out of a lock is to buy something.

Counter. Close it and say stop. Do the sum in Numbers before you send anything. The least a tool owes you is a free stop that always works.

Tactics, and how to refuse them
What they say or doThe counter
You’re special, I care about all my subs, you don’t have to sendJudge a dom by what she actually asks for and rewards. If everyone sends anyway, the reassurance means nothing.
I could make you send a fortune with one word, and I won’t, because I careThe power is there whether she uses it or not. Trust built like this is dependence.
The doms you paid before are losersRate a dom by how she treats you, and ignore how she rates others.
Praise, titles and new projects for work you do for freeAgree in advance what you’ll do, and keep the right to say no without losing your place.
A short firm command, a finished example that makes it look easy, then the same command again when you hesitateIf you hesitate, there’s a reason. Sleep on it, and decide in your own time.
Hand over your public account so it can show your sendsNever. Your account is your identity.

These lines are common in the scene. They’re here so you can see them coming.

Scene drama reaches subs too. Feuds spill into subs’ accounts, and a call-out can be one dom using other people to hurt another. A rumour can spread private screenshots to dozens of people before anyone checks. Supporter names show on public wishlists, and doms who are friends look at each other’s pages. Assume the name you use is known across the scene. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

What a serious dom shows

Before she asks for money, a serious dom shows you her rules: her limits, what she throws out, the plain truth about prices and a clear way to stop. She tells a sub ahead of time when he mustn’t send, and what happens then. If money meant for someone else reaches her, she passes it on at once and says so. She checks availability before she promises a gift. A dom who hides these has told you what she is. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Whatever a sub tells her about his money, a serious dom doesn’t use it to ask for more. I’ve heard people argue that most subs who say they’re broke are lying. If that’s wrong, he’s the one who pays for it. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Some makers disguise what a payment page really is. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. I don’t describe how any of that is built. A processor that catches it can freeze the funds, a sub’s money included. UnverifiedPlausible, not proven yet. If your bank says no, that’s your answer. A real dom never needs a workaround.

If any of this sounds familiar, set a limit while you’re calm, keep a stop that works at once, and wait before you raise either. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. If sending already feels hard to stop, read The spiral and Where to get help.

Check yourself

  1. Question 1 of 2A dom’s follower list is long. What does it tell you about her income?

    Answer. Very little, because followers can be other doms or people who never send. Income comes from a handful of people, and a long list can hide how few they are.

  2. Question 2 of 2A dom says she could make you send a fortune with one word, and that she won’t because she cares. What do you do?

    Answer. Notice that the power is there whether or not she uses it. Showing power and then holding back builds trust and dependence at the same time.
