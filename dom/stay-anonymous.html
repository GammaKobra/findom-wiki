A drawn face can’t leak, but files, names, accounts, tools and payments can. Here’s where the person behind a persona gets exposed, where anonymity ends, and why access to your accounts is never part of the game.

Step 1 of 6 What you hide, from whom

This chapter is not advice for your situation.

In 30 seconds

  • A drawn persona is anonymous toward the audience. She isn’t anonymous toward the payout.
  • Only the persona’s name belongs in anything you produce. Clean every file, then check it.
  • Small public fields, game names and wishlist names connect accounts.
  • A real dom never needs a password, a login code or a remote session.
  • If it leaks, stop, secure, keep proof and ask for help.

You will learn

  • Who can find out who you are, and how.
  • How to clean files and sites, and how to check a tool before you run it.
  • Which public fields and names give a person away, for doms and for subs.
  • Why anonymity ends where the money lands.
  • What to do when a login request, a threat or a leak shows up.

I keep the persona and the person apart on purpose, and the person’s side is the one I check most often.

What you hide, from whom

Anonymity in 2DFD is real, but narrow. It works well toward the audience: a drawn face can’t leak, a clean file carries no coordinates, and an account under a pseudonym shows no name. Compared with classic findom, the drawn character takes the face and the voice out of the deal, and the risk moves to payment platforms, social platforms and the tools used to move money. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Some doms say they’ve been targeted by people threatening them with fake explicit pictures made to look like them. Those threats are scary even when the pictures are known to be fake. A drawn character has no real face to fake, and that’s real protection. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Secrecy has a cost. In a study of findommes, participants named secrecy among the most negative parts of the work. Files, sites and tools

Only the persona’s name goes in anything you produce: files, file names, commits, metadata and screenshots. Never a personal name, an e-mail address or a system path that contains a user name. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Images carry traces: location data, colour profiles, comments, provenance records, prompts stored in text chunks and extra bytes. Videos carry a date, the name of the program and the name of the encoder. Clean every export, then reread the file, and if anything’s left, don’t post it. Clean even when you start clean, because an encoder can add a profile on its own. Test your checker with odd cases, because a hidden note in an unexpected spot once slipped through mine. Treat every picture as copyable too, since anyone can take a screenshot. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Publish an image

  1. 1

    Export the file from your tool.

  2. 2

    Strip it: location, profiles, comments, prompts and extra bytes.

  3. 3

    Reread the stripped file and compare it with a clean sample.

  4. 4

    If anything’s left, stop and fix the cause, not the file.

  5. 5

    Only then post it.

A site can leak through how it’s built. Serve everything from the site’s own host, with no third-party fonts, trackers or scripts, so only the host sees a visit. Check it from the outside: old versions of a site can stay reachable at hosting addresses you’ve forgotten. Browser safety filters can flag new adult-themed domains early, so test the name and keep a backup. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Code leaks too. Make commits under the persona’s name and in UTC, because a local time offset shows where the author lives. Open a code-hosting account with a pseudonym for both the name and the e-mail, because every change stays in the history. Before you give a tool to others, take out any name, signature and default picture, and strip the source, comments and leftover handles, or someone can repackage it with something added. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Secrets never go through a chat. Type a token or key into a masked prompt in your own terminal, never into a commit, screenshot or message, and send access keys privately so they can be revoked. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

A tool can be harmless in code and still unsafe in practice. Check what it sends and to whom, check its defaults as well as its screen, and test it in a virtual machine with no personal network. Don’t run an unknown program on your main computer. Avoid any tool that builds an ID from the computer name or the account name. A safer design starts with everything off, a whitelist, a random ID, a short history and a control channel by invitation only. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Anonymous confession features are only anonymous toward other members. The platform and the host still have the data. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Accounts, names and public fields

Public fields leak more than files. One careless bio line on a related account can show that the person behind a persona isn’t who the persona says. A game or stream name that looks like the persona’s name, or points back to an old dispute, can do the same. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Read every public field of every linked account the way a stranger would.

People who run personas often have several accounts. Platform terms may forbid multiple accounts, or a new account after a ban, and a second account puts the first one at risk. UnverifiedPlausible, not proven yet. Don’t borrow someone else’s phone number for a check. It drags a person who has nothing to do with this into adult activity. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Don’t confirm or explain links between personas. When someone asks about a persona’s contacts or second identities, deflect. Never publicly link a handle you found in a tool’s files to a persona unless the person said so in their own words, because that’s doxxingdoxTo expose someone’s real identity or address. It’s the main safety fear behind anonymity in the scene, so a request for a real name, or for a gift that needs a physical address, is a risk. It can hit the person behind a persona and the sub alike.. Exposing a bad actor also invites revenge, like mass reports from accounts the rival controls. If you show a mentor or a peer anything, show the persona account, never the personal one. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Purging messages and blocking contacts keeps the persona apart from the person, but it also wipes out what you could learn from. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Deleting chats after every send is different, because it can be a sign of the spiralspiralTwo senses. First, a rotating pattern, the typical hypnotic image of the scene. Second, the spending spiral, where spending that started as play tightens turn by turn, from curious to chasing. If you regret it and keep spending, that’s an early warning, and every turn of the spending spiral has a way out, which Where to get help helps you find.. See The spiral.

If you’re a sub

Sender names can show on a public wishlist, and doms look at each other’s pages. Use a handle you’re fine with several doms seeing. Keep a separate account for adult servers, and check who else is in a server before you join, because a server you have in common with someone can expose you. On a shared computer, lock the screen or close the chat, and use a separate profile. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Your name, your address, your workplace and your family are a fair boundary. A dom who needs them isn’t playing a game. Physical gifts break anonymity both ways, so the safe default is no addresses at all, or a neutral forwarding or retail service. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Money is the weak point

Anonymity ends at the payout. In my experience a card processor or an adult payment company ties the persona to a legal identity: ID documents, a business registration and a bank account in the company’s country. An account opened in a real name carries any ban with it, and the ban follows the real person. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

The persona is a mask toward the audience and the platforms, never toward the company that pays you. Partial or false details at a processor won’t make you anonymous. They get accounts closed and money held, and a sub’s payment can disappear with them. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. The authors of a study of findommes flag fraud, including identity fraud, as a remaining risk in this field. VVivian’s field notes

I track how these requests are worded. The language of trust and the language of a scam overlap completely. When someone says a password proves you’re serious, the request itself is the red flag, whatever they mean by it. A dom who means well doesn’t need your password, and one who doesn’t will say the same words.

Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Red flags

Access requests

  • A dom asks for your password as proof you’re honest.
  • A dom asks for the one-time code just sent to your phone or e-mail.
  • A dom asks for a remote desktop session “to supervise”, and adds that you can always quit.
  • A dom logs you out of your own accounts, or keeps them open “for you”.
  • A dom asks for access as the price of forgiveness.

Counter. Say no once, calmly. Offer a screenshot or an export instead. If you already shared anything, change the password and sign out of every session. Don’t argue.

When it leaks

Rumours and screenshots travel fast. A rumour that a dom is secretly a sub can cost her subs, and private images can spread through a shared server before any apology comes. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Keep private images and chats out of any shared space.

If your identity leaks

  1. 1

    Stop. Don’t confirm or deny any real-life detail.

  2. 2

    Secure your accounts: change passwords and sign out of every session.

  3. 3

    Save proof with dates before you block anyone.

  4. 4

    Report the account to the platform. Don’t pay a blackmailer, and don’t send more material.

  5. 5

    Tell one person you trust, and read Where to get help.
