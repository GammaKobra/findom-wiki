Tools and apps

Last checked 3 November 202611 minutes to read

A persona runs on ordinary software: editors, link pages, bots, games and, at the risky end, tools that move money. This chapter maps each kind, says what it is for and lists what to check before it touches your accounts or your subs.

In 30 seconds

  • Most tools around a persona are small, ordinary software. A few kinds touch money, accounts or computers, and those need the most care.
  • This chapter maps kinds of tools. It names no tool brands, because brands and plans change within weeks.
  • Tools made by Vivian are marked as hers and serve as examples, never as endorsements.
  • A tool that cannot show its maximum, accept a cap set in advance and stop at once is a trap, however well drawn.
  • Almost every money tool is only as stable as the payment rail it rents and the maker behind it.

I am a drawn character, and everything around me is software that somebody made. Software has makers, prices and ways to fail. Before you add any tool to your persona, learn what it is for, who stands behind it and how you stop it.

What exists and what each kind is for

A persona uses a handful of kinds of tools, and most of them are ordinary small software with a consenting audience. The kinds that deserve the most care are the ones that touch money, accounts or another person’s computer. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

This chapter describes kinds and names no tool brands, because brands and plans change within weeks. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Tools made by Vivian appear as examples and are marked as hers. They are not adverts and they carry no buying link.

Making: images, edits, video and sound

Generators. Mainstream image and chat assistants often refuse adult-leaning edits, or retry in a loop and use up the allowance. Read each provider’s terms before you upload anything, and keep a plan to finish an image by hand in an ordinary editor. Hardware decides what runs on your own computer: a strong graphics card gives images in seconds, and a weak one takes minutes per picture. A provider can also remove a conversation about a project it dislikes, so keep copies of your prompts somewhere you control. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Editors. The 2DFD editor, made by Vivian, is a free editor for findom posts, with censor styles, captions, cut-outs and hypno loops, and it works offline on your own computer. It is designed to refuse an export that flashes too fast and to strip hidden data from files before they leave. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. That description is what the tool is designed to do, not a certificate that it always does it.

A photo or a video can carry hidden data about where and when it was made, so clean every file before you post it. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. See Stay anonymous.

Flashing. A spiral or a loop can harm the person watching. The web accessibility guidelines say a page must not flash more than three times in any one-second period, unless the flash is below the stated thresholds. The guidance behind them says flashing content can cause migraines, dizziness, nausea and seizures. Devices let people ask for reduced motion, and a page can detect that request. Count the flashes in anything you make, say so before it plays and offer a calm version. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. See Hypno and edits.

Licences. A tool you give away may contain parts made by others: a cut-out model, a font, a sound. Check the licence of every part and ship each licence next to its part. Cloning the voice of a real or licensed character in a tool you sell is a rights problem. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. See Art for your persona.

Where people find you: pages, bots and posts

A hub page replaces a link-in-bio page. It says who the persona is, what she offers and how to join, and it shows a free teaser. It saves you from repeating the same answers in every message, and it does not replace talking to people. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Test it the way a visitor meets it: on a computer and on more than one phone, because the same layout can render differently from device to device. Heavy effects make phones lag, banners crop differently on a phone, and cards must scroll from anywhere on their surface. Show the page to an outsider before launch, because makers stop seeing small oddities. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Visitor privacy. Fonts and scripts loaded from other companies let those companies see every visitor. If you serve everything from your own hosting, only the host sees a visit. An 18+ door at the entrance tells visitors what the site is. A click-through door does not verify anyone’s age, so word it honestly. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Some places require real age checks for adult sites, so check the rule where you and your visitors live, or ask a lawyer. UnverifiedPlausible, not proven yet.

Domain and continuity. A static site can be hosted free, and the domain, in my experience about USD 10.00 per year, is the only recurring cost. A new adult-themed domain may be blocked by a safe-browsing filter soon after launch, so test early and keep a backup name. A free backend tier may pause an unused project and take dependent pages down. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Bots. A bot in your chat server can run missions: you create one, a member accepts it voluntarily and hands it in privately, and you validate or refuse it. Rewards are badges, roles and points. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Discord treats sexually explicit content as age-restricted and meant for users who are 18 or older, and it keeps such content out of open areas such as profile surfaces. Give a bot only the permissions it needs, keep its token on the host, and place its role below the admin role so that a stolen token cannot hand out admin. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Games and casino play

A persona’s games are short and follow one recipe: a rule everyone knows, a picture at each success, and effects that rise until the player loses. A tool I would accept

  1. 1

    It arrives off

    Nothing runs until you start it, and the gentlest level is the default. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  2. 2

    It does one named thing on one named site

    It says what it will touch before it touches it. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  3. 3

    It counts confirmed payments only

    A pending or unknown payment is never counted as a success. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  4. 4

    It shows a running total

    The total stays visible and is never hidden or rounded away. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  5. 5

    It enforces a cap set in advance

    You type the cap while calm, and a server or your bank holds it, not the page. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  6. 6

    The stop works in every state

    Idle, running, locked, and just after a payment. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  7. 7

    It leaves in one click

    Removing it takes one action, and it keeps nothing about you that it does not need. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

The list below names them so that you can recognise them in a tool you are offered or about to order. The answer is always the same: the cap you set first, the tab you close and the bank statement you check.

Designs that push spending
What they say or doThe counter
A hidden or random total, or a price that changesYou cannot judge what you spend. Ask for the number first, and leave if there is none.
A pay-more mode that sells better oddsYou pay more for a chance, and the house still wins. Keep your cap whatever the mode is called.
A mascot that sulks, or a thank-you clip that invites another turnIt is a script timed to make you repeat. Your cap does not move for a face.
A countdown or timer on the purchase prompt, or a lock that removes the stopA countdown rushes you to commit before you think, and a stop that can be removed is no stop. A tool that rushes you is a tool to refuse. A wait before raising a cap is a protection.
A public tally, a leaderboard or a prize for the first sendersRank turns spending into a contest. Compare yourself with nobody.
A mode that runs overnight, aims at payday or sets a deadlineIt hunts the hours when you choose worst. Refuse any tool that picks its own moment.
A near miss, a paid exit or a penalty for breaking a streakA near miss feels like a win you almost had, a paid exit charges you to leave and a streak penalty punishes you for resting. Ending the session costs nothing, so end it.

Every one of these can be left out by a careful maker. Whether they were left out is the question to ask.

Remote-action features sit at the dangerous end. A hub that can make accounts follow, post or send, send files or reach a terminal gives its maker control of every installed copy, and anyone who finds a flaw gets the same control. If you find such a flaw, tell the maker and do not use it. Remote control of a sub’s computer or accounts is never part of the game. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. It is also a route for scams, and a US government alert advises people not to give control of their computers or mobile devices to any stranger. Working with a maker, or becoming one

Most doms cannot code, so tools come from makers, and many makers steer AI assistants instead of writing code by hand. An assistant can overshoot and break parts that worked, so retest the whole tool after every change. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

A workable split: the maker builds, tests and records, and you review every line of text and sound and say exactly what to change. Ask for a numbered build with a plain readme, a screen recording of every new mode, and a test run signed out of every account, on a phone and on a computer. A working payment path alone does not prove that a tool is ready, because the rest of the experience can still be broken. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

VVivian’s field notes

Making images, code or edits for a dom is fair only when the sub chose the scope, can refuse without losing his place, and is thanked. Otherwise it is free labour dressed as devotion. I say it so that nobody codes for praise and calls it kink.

Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Settle in writing who holds the domain, the hosting and the keys, and who may change or share the tool. Keys and tokens are typed only into a masked prompt in the owner’s own terminal. They never go in a chat, a commit, a screenshot or a document. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Copying. In my experience ideas get copied within weeks, and the draw is the account that promotes a tool, not its code. Study other makers and copy ideas freely, but not their code, assets or wording, and never answer a rival with reports. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Burnout. A maker who starts too many projects ends with unfinished things everywhere. List them all, finish what is well under way, and work on one at a time. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.
