Techdom

Last checked 3 November 20268 minutes to read

Techdom is domination carried out by tools: software that sends, likes, locks or takes over, with the sub’s agreement and a stop he controls. This chapter says what it is, who wants it, and where consent ends.

VerifiedChecked against the original source.ReportedStated by a serious secondary source read in full.Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

This chapter is not advice for your situation.

In 30 seconds

  • Techdom means a tool does part of the dominating, with the sub’s agreement given beforehand and a stop he can use at any moment.
  • A tool can take three things from you: money, attention or control of your machine. The further down that list, the more you hand over.
  • Doms like it because it earns while they are away. Subs like it because it removes the decision, and that is also its main danger.
  • Remote control of your computer is the highest-risk offer, and the request follows the pattern of a common scam.
  • Trust a tool for its visible limits, never for the dom’s word.

You will learn

  • What techdom means in this wiki, and which tools it covers.
  • Why doms build tools and why subs ask for them.
  • What a remote session really hands over, and how to refuse one.
  • What consent looks like inside a tool, and which pressure designs to refuse.

A machine can hold the rules, and that is the only version of this I would trust: an amount, a limit and a way out, written down where nobody can bend them. When a tool lacks those three, it is a trap with a theme, and this chapter is here so you can tell the two apart.

What techdom is

techdomtechdomDominance expressed through technology, such as extensions, apps or tools, so that the dom earns without live effort each time. It is a game between consenting adults, always with a cap, a wait and a stop that the sub controls. Remote access is never part of the game. is the part of 2DFD where a tool does the dominating. The dom sets the rules, and software on the sub’s own computer, phone or accounts carries them out. Typical examples are an extension that sends gifts by itself and one that likes and reposts the dom’s posts from the sub’s own account. Others are an app that sets his wallpaper or locks his device, and a live session in which the dom drives his screen. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Some creators use the word more loosely, for any corner of the scene where a person builds something with technology instead of only talking. This wiki uses the narrow meaning, because it is the one that can be made safe: a tool acts for the dom, the sub agreed beforehand, and a stop that he controls works at every moment. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Handing a decision to someone else and to a machine is a fantasy that adults may choose. Done well, a machine can show the rules in numbers. Done badly, the machine removes the pause in which a person changes his mind. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

Why doms build it and why subs ask for it

For a dom, a tool lets subs pay without her being present. Chasing sends around the clock can earn more, but it costs all of her time. A dom may also find it exciting to wake up to a batch of silent sends that arrived overnight. For some doms, watching a sub send is itself the kink, and a live chat can run beside a tool that sends in the background. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

For a sub, giving up the decision is the point. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. Once he presses start, he no longer chooses. Sessions through a screen

A remote session is sold as a private session. The dom connects to the sub’s computer, sometimes for hours, and may change his wallpaper, watch him play, or act as him on his accounts. The usual request is that the sub approves full control and blocks his own keyboard and mouse, removing his ability to interrupt. Everything open on the machine is in view: files, saved passwords, mail and chats. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

This is the highest-risk offer in techdom, and it matches a known fraud pattern. Public agencies advise people not to give control of a computer or phone to a stranger. Red flags

A remote-access request is a red flag when

  • It comes from someone you met recently, or late in a long, warm chat.
  • It arrives as a joke, a dare or a test of trust.
  • It asks you to read out a connection code, or to install software you did not look up.
  • It asks you to block your own keyboard and mouse.
  • It will run while you are away from the screen.

Counter. Say no to the remote part and offer screenshots, or a shared screen that you can end yourself. They serve almost every purpose, and nobody who plays fair needs more.

Refusing costs nothing, and these steps keep the stop in your hands.

How to refuse a remote session

  1. 1

    Say no to remote control in plain words, however warm the chat has been. Nobody who plays fair needs it.

  2. 2

    Offer screenshots instead, or a shared screen that you start, limit to one window and end yourself.

  3. 3

    Never install remote-access software because someone asks, and never read out a connection code.

  4. 4

    Never agree to block your own keyboard and mouse. Blocked input removes your stop.

  5. 5

    If you already gave access, end the connection yourself, remove the program, change any password that was visible on screen, and read Where to get help.

What consent looks like in a tool

Consent to a tool is set before the session, in plain terms, and can be withdrawn. Agreement given in the moment is weak: one press account asks whether it is really consent when a client is too aroused to think straight about taking on debt. ReportedStated by a serious secondary source read in full.Diary of a Financial Dominatrix, part one (opens in a new tab) Accessed 3 November 2026. Consent covers only what the sub knew the tool would do, and a tool that does more has gone beyond the agreement.

Access is never part of the game. Press interviews note that some doms ask for debt contracts or control of a sub’s bank accounts. ReportedStated by a serious secondary source read in full.The Curse of the Fake Financial Dominatrix (opens in a new tab) Accessed 3 November 2026. A tool can be a quicker version of the same hand-over.

Trust should rest on design, not on the dom’s word. Whoever runs a tool can act on what it reaches at any moment. A trustworthy techdom shows visible limits, an emergency stop, a record of what the tool can see, and nothing that can act without the sub. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study. For a browser extension, the Chrome Details page shows which sites it may read and change, so you can look before you press start. VerifiedChecked against the original source.Install and manage extensions - Chrome Web Store Help (opens in a new tab) Accessed 3 November 2026. The full check is in Check a tool before you install it.

A tool that respects consent usually has these marks:

  • It arrives switched off, and it does one named thing on one named site.
  • You set the cap before you start, and something other than the page you are looking at enforces it.
  • A running total is always visible, and a payment counts only when it is confirmed.
  • A stop is always on screen, works in every state of the tool, and costs nothing.
  • Any control program has a quick exit, such as a shortcut or a button, and a safety word agreed beforehand.
  • A command that you did not accept inside the program never runs.
  • A trigger, such as a sound that you were taught to obey, has an agreed way to be switched off.

The design rules behind this list are in Safe design.

Myths about tools

  • Myth

    A techdom tool only affects my computer.

    Fact

    A tool that acts through your accounts or your card reaches the services tied to them. Your social account can be limited, your bank sees every payment, and a remote session reaches whatever is open. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  • Myth

    If I trust the dom, I do not need limits.

    Fact

    Trust in a person is not a limit. A cap, a wait and a stop work whether or not the person deserves your trust. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  • Myth

    Silent sends mean a happy sub.

    Fact

    A recurring send with no message may be a tool running unwatched. Check in before you enjoy it. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.

  • Myth

    I can always close the program, so I need no stop.

    Fact

    Closing the window is the last resort. A tool needs a visible stop, a pause and a cap of its own. Vivian’s field notesFirst-hand experience, not a study.
